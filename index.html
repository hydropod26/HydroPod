The discovery and the basic idea

Four thousand metres down, in total darkness, metal-rich rocks on the seabed appear to make oxygen. HydroPod turns that finding into a working harvester.

Deep-sea geobatteries

Across the abyssal plains, about 4,000 m below the surface, lie fields of polymetallic nodules: potato-sized lumps of manganese, nickel and cobalt that grow over millions of years.

The metal-oxide surface of each nodule holds a natural electrical potential. Packed side by side, nodules behave like natural batteries, with roughly 1.2 V available to the seawater around them. That potential is one proposed explanation for “dark oxygen”: oxygen made where no sunlight can reach.

Natural potential falls short. At 1.2 V the nodules sit 0.3 V below the 1.5 V needed to split water, so electrolysis never gets going.

Synthetic Nodule Catalyst Grid

A lattice of lab-made nodules, built from manganese, nickel and cobalt oxides, is laid on the seabed. Their textured, nodule-like surface exposes more active catalyst per square metre, which is where the 16.4% biomimetic gain comes from.
